title: Ultra-Strongly Self-Interacting Dark Matter and the Origin of Little Red Dots
---

With the advent of the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), observational probes of the structure of objects in the early universe have become increasingly accessible. In particular, the discovery of high-redshift (*z*&nbsp;∼&nbsp;10) supermassive black holes (SMBHs) challenges conventional formation channels, which struggle to produce sufficiently massive seeds or grow them within the available timescales without prolonged periods of super-Eddington accretion. Similarly, in the past few years, a new class of high-redshift objects has been discovered, known as “Little Red Dots” (LRDs), many of which show evidence for accreting SMBHs (10<sup>6</sup>–&#8288;10<sup>8</sup>&nbsp;M<sub>⊙</sub>) hosted in highly compact galaxies approximately 100–200 parsecs in size.

To address the formation of these objects, we have introduced a model of self-interacting dark matter (SIDM), in which a small fraction of the dark matter is ultra-strongly self-interacting (uSIDM). This simple dark-sector model realizes both a SIDM and a uSIDM component while reproducing the observed dark matter relic abundance. Typical uSIDM cross-sections are on the order of 10<sup>3</sup>–&#8288;10<sup>4</sup>&nbsp;cm<sup>2</sup>/g, several orders of magnitude larger than those typically considered for SIDM. With such high cross-sections, the uSIDM undergoes rapid gravothermal evolution leading to a full core-collapse of the innermost portion of the dark matter halo. The rapid timescale for this collapse allows for the formation of a heavy SMBH seed well within the available formation timescales; thus uSIDM is a natural candidate for the formation of LRDs. Subsequent sub-Eddington accretion grows the seeds to reproduce the inferred SMBH masses and LRD mass function, while also making a falsifiable prediction for the clustering bias of LRDs at *z*&nbsp;∼&nbsp;5. Finally, I will introduce our recently developed multi-component gravothermal solver, designed to investigate the coupled evolution of dark matter components and the dynamics of core-collapse, and discuss how recent developments in gravothermal modeling may impact our understanding of early SMBH formation.
